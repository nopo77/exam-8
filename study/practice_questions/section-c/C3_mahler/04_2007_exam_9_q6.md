# 2007 Exam 9 - Q6

**Points:** 2

## Question

The actuary for an insurance company has been asked by senior management to determine whether the company's expected frequency has been shifting over time.

The actuary knows that the company has maintained a constant number of exposures and a uniform mix of business since 1997.

Based on an assumption that expected frequency has remained constant during the period, the actuary has compiled the following data.

| B | C | D | E | F | G | H | I | J | K | L |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Year | 1,997 | 1,998 | 1,999 | 2,000 | 2,001 | 2,002 | 2,003 | 2,004 | 2,005 | 2,006 |
| Actual Claims | 475 | 420 | 460 | 500 | 490 | 525 | 515 | 510 | 540 | 575 |
| Expected Claims | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 |

Discuss two methods that the actuary could use to test whether the expected frequency has been shifting over time. Describe any assumptions, calculations, or additional information that would be necessary to completely formulate and carry out each test.

## Solution

Note that in the source paper, Mahler describes the chi-squared test applying to each single risk (baseball team) separately. In this problem, we will use it to test a single insurer. For the correlations test, the discrepancy with the source material is more of an issue. The source material focuses on the correlations of individual risks within a class over time (i.e., are risks that are better/worse than average for 1 time period also likely to be better/worse than average in subsequent time periods). That is a bit different than looking at whether there is correlation between the experience of an individual risk (or insurer in this case) with the prior experience of that same individual risk.

When this was tested on paper, it would have been very time consuming to perform the calculations for these tests in addition to discussing them. But with the exam in spreadsheet format, it is much quicker to perform calculations, so I'll show 2 solutions, with and without calculations.

Solution 1: No calculations, answer the question as originally asked

Method 1: Chi-Squared Test

If needed for stability, group data (e.g., claim counts) into n equally-sized time intervals (e.g., 5 year intervals). For each interval, calculate chi-squared = (A-E)^2 / E; where A = actual counts for interval, E = expected counts for interval Sum up chi-squared across all n intervals. Compare the total chi-squared value against a chi-squared table value for the appropriate degrees of freedom (n-1) and confidence level. If total chi-squared value > table value, then conclude actual and expected distributions are different, and frequency has shifted over time. If total chi-squared value < table value, then conclude actual and expected distributions are the same, and frequency has not shifted over time.

Method 2: Correlations Test

Calculate the correlation for t = 1, i.e., the correlation between the insurer's history and the insurer's history lagged 1 year. Repeat the prior step for all values of t. Now look at whether the correlations decrease for larger values of t, and if so, then we conclude frequency is shifting over time.
