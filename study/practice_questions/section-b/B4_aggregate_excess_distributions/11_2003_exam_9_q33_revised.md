# 2003 Exam 9 - Q33 revised

**Points:** 2

## Question

You are helping an underwriter price a retrospectively-rated workers compensation policy with a per occurrence limit on losses contributing to the aggregate limit. The policy specifies no minimum aggregate limit. Between last year and this year, the customer has doubled its work force without changing the nature of what it does. Assume that this is the only change between the expiring and renewing policy (i.e. assume no inflation, trends, or benefit changes; also assume no changes to selected limits or deductibles).

How would each of the following change, if at all, due to the increase in exposure? Explain your answers.

### Part a (1.00 pts)

Limited Table M Insurance charge, as a percentage of expected losses.

### Part b (1.00 pts)

Excess ratio (excess losses as a percentage of expected ground-up losses).

## Solution

### Part a

There are 2 opposing impacts in this question. The CAS accepted answers stating an increase or a decrease. There are 2 opposing pieces at work here. I'll use an answer that addresses both pieces.

The expected loss E[A] will increase (presumably it will double based on doubling the exposure). This may cause the risk to shift to a different, more stable aggregate distribution in Limited Table M, which would have lower charges for a given entry ratio. However, since the aggregate limit L_G is not changing, the entry ratio of the limit divided by the now higher expected losses would be much lower, causing the charge to increase. It is unclear which of these opposing pieces will dominate, but it is more likely for the entry ratio decrease based on doubling the exposure to outweigh the impact of the shift in columns.

### Part b

This should not change since the per occurrence limit is not changing, and the changes in exposure do not make it more likely to have higher individual claims.

You could also make an argument that the charge should be higher in this part if the additional work force contributed to a higher concentration of risk. For example, if an earthquake were to occur, that would be a single occurrence, and if there are more workers around, it is more likely that the occurrence limit would be hit with additional workers that could be injured.
